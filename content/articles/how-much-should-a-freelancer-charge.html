Ask ten freelancers how they set their rate and nine will describe the same method: they looked at what they used to earn, divided it by something, and adjusted until the number stopped feeling embarrassing. It is the most expensive habit in self-employment, and it is entirely fixable.

The three bad methods

1. Salary divided by 2,080

You earned $75,000 at your last job, there are 2,080 working hours in a year, so $36 an hour. This is wrong in four directions at once. Your employer paid for your holidays, your sick days, half your social security contributions, your health insurance, your equipment, your software and the hours you spent in meetings. None of that was in the $75,000, and all of it is now your problem.

2. Matching the competition

You found three other freelancers charging $50 an hour, so you charge $50. But you have no idea whether those three are profitable, how many hours they bill, what their costs are, or whether they are quietly going broke. Copying a price copies someone else's cost structure, which you cannot see.

3. Whatever the client will tolerate

Tempting, and occasionally lucrative, but it has no floor. Without knowing the rate below which work costs you money, you will eventually accept a project that loses you money while feeling busy and successful.

The method that works: price backwards

Start from the life you want and work back to the rate that funds it. There are five numbers.

  1. What you want to take home. Not revenue — the money that reaches your personal account after tax.
  2. What it costs to run your business. Software subscriptions, hardware, insurance, accountant, coworking, phone, internet, courses, bank fees. Add it up honestly; it is usually between $4,000 and $15,000 a year for a solo freelancer.
  3. Your tax rate. Income tax plus self-employment or national insurance contributions.
  4. Hours you can genuinely bill. Not 40 a week. After sales calls, proposals, invoicing, bookkeeping, email and learning, 20–27 is the realistic range.
  5. A profit buffer. Ten percent, for the invoices that arrive late and the projects that run over.

Put those five numbers into the hourly rate calculator and it does the arithmetic, including the part most people get wrong — grossing up for tax rather than subtracting it.

The grossing-up trap

This single error costs freelancers thousands a year. If you want to keep $60,000 and you lose 28% to tax, you do not need $60,000 plus 28%. You need:

$60,000 ÷ (1 − 0.28) = $83,333, not $76,800.

The gap is $6,533. Spread across 1,100 billable hours, that is nearly $6 an hour you forgot to charge for, every hour, forever.

What the number usually comes out as

For a freelancer wanting a $60,000 take-home, with $6,000 of costs, a 28% tax rate and 25 billable hours a week, the answer lands near $87 an hour. People are routinely shocked by this, because it is two and a half times the naive salary-divided-by-2,080 figure. Both numbers describe the same standard of living. Only one of them is survivable.

Then stop selling hours

Once you have the rate, use it as a private measuring stick rather than a public price. Hourly billing has a structural flaw: the better you get, the faster you finish, and the less you earn. Fixed-fee quoting removes that. Use the project quote calculator to convert an hours estimate into a price that includes complexity, revisions and a deposit — then quote the price and never mention the hours.

What to say when a client pushes back

They will, and it is not personal. Three responses that work better than discounting:

  • “I can work to that budget — here is what I would take out of scope.” Price and scope move together, always. The moment they move independently, your first number was fiction.
  • “That is my rate for this kind of work. For a longer engagement I can offer X.” Volume earns a discount. A single project does not.
  • Silence. After you state a price, stop talking. The urge to fill the pause with a justification or a discount is where most negotiations are lost.

Raise it every year

Your costs rise annually. If your rate does not, you take a real-terms pay cut every single year without ever deciding to. A 5–8% increase on renewal is normal, expected, and almost never contested if you give notice. The freelancers who struggle most are rarely the ones who charged too much; they are the ones who charged the same thing for six years.

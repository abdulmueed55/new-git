Why hours × rate is not a quote

An estimate of 40 hours at $90 gives you $3,600. Then the client asks for a third round of changes, the brand assets arrive in the wrong format, and two stakeholders disagree about the homepage. You deliver in 58 hours and earned $62 an hour, not $90.

A quote is not a prediction of hours. It is a price for an outcome, carrying the risk that the outcome takes longer than planned. Everything this calculator adds on top of base hours is a price for that risk.

What each input is doing

Complexity multiplier

Work you have done fifty times is genuinely cheaper for you to deliver than work you have never done. Unfamiliar domains, legacy systems, five-stakeholder approval chains and vague briefs all inflate the real hours in ways your estimate cannot see. Be honest here — it is the single biggest lever on whether a project is profitable.

Revision buffer

Twenty percent is a sensible default for two rounds of feedback. If you have not written a revision limit into your contract, use more. The sentence that saves careers is: “The quote includes two rounds of revisions; further rounds are billed at my hourly rate.”

Rush fee

A rush job is not just overtime, it is displacement — you are pushing other clients back to fit it in. 25% to 50% is standard. Charging nothing teaches clients that your calendar is free.

Deposit

Forty to fifty percent before work starts is normal professional practice, not an insult. It funds the work, it filters out clients who were never going to pay, and it means a disappearing client costs you half a project rather than all of it. If an invoice does go unpaid, the late fee calculator works out what they owe.

A worked example

A 40-hour website build at $90 an hour, somewhat unfamiliar (1.25×), with a 20% revision buffer and $200 of stock assets:

Note the effective hourly figure the calculator shows. It is the rate you earn if the pessimistic case happens — complexity and revisions both land in full. If that number is below your target rate, the quote is too low no matter how good $5,600 sounds.

How to present it

  • Send one number. An itemised breakdown invites line-by-line negotiation. Keep the breakdown for yourself.
  • Offer three options, not one price. A reduced scope, the recommended scope and an expanded scope. Clients who are given three options choose between them instead of choosing whether to hire you.
  • Never discount without removing scope. If the price drops, something must come out. Otherwise you have taught the client that your first number was not real.
  • Put the expiry date on it. “This quote is valid for 30 days” prevents a client from accepting last spring's price next autumn.

Frequently asked questions

Should I show the client my hourly rate?

Generally no. Once a client knows your hourly rate, every conversation becomes about hours rather than outcomes, and efficiency starts to look like overcharging. Quote the project. Keep the rate as the private tool you use to check the quote is worth doing.

What if I estimate the hours badly?

Everyone does, almost always downward. Two defences: the complexity multiplier here, and a written scope that says exactly what is included. Over time, track actual hours against your estimates — most freelancers find a personal correction factor somewhere between 1.3 and 1.8 and start applying it automatically.

Is a 40% deposit really normal?

Yes, across design, development, writing and consulting. Common structures are 50/50, 40/30/30 across milestones, or 30% up front with monthly billing on longer engagements. The clients who object loudest to deposits are, reliably, the clients who later pay late.

How do I handle scope creep after the quote is accepted?

With a change order, not with resentment. “Happy to add that — it is about six hours, so $540, and it moves delivery to the 14th. Shall I send a revised quote?” Said early and without drama, this almost never damages a relationship; silently absorbing the work always damages the project.

Does the calculator store my quotes?

No. Everything runs in your browser and disappears when you close the tab. Use the copy button to paste the breakdown into your own notes or proposal.

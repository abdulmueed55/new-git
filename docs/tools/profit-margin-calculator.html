Margin and markup, in one table

Both describe the same profit. They divide it by different things.

Margin = profit ÷ selling price
Markup = profit ÷ cost

A job costs you $600 and you sell it for $1,000. The profit is $400. That is a 40% margin ($400 ÷ $1,000) and a 66.7% markup ($400 ÷ $600). Same transaction, two very different percentages.

The mistake, and what it costs

A business decides it needs a 40% margin. It adds 40% to cost and moves on. On a $600 cost that gives $840, and $240 of profit on $840 is a 28.6% margin, not 40%. The shortfall is $160 on every single job.

Across a hundred jobs a year, the business is $16,000 poorer than it believes it is, and its owner cannot work out why the healthy-looking percentages never turn into money in the bank. The correct operation is to divide:

Price for a target margin = cost ÷ (1 − margin)
$600 ÷ (1 − 0.40) = $1,000

Which one should you actually use?

Use markup to set prices, because you start from cost and multiply. Use margin to judge the health of the business, because it tells you what share of your revenue you keep and compares cleanly across products, clients and competitors. Trouble only starts when a number calculated one way gets reported as the other.

What counts as “cost” for a service business?

For a freelancer there is no inventory, so cost is your time plus anything you buy for the job. Value your time at your real hourly rate — the one from the hourly rate calculator, which already includes tax, overheads and unpaid hours. Then add subcontractors, stock assets, licences and travel.

If you value your time at zero, every job looks profitable and the business is slowly eating you. That is the single most common accounting error in freelancing, and it never shows up in a spreadsheet — only in exhaustion.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between margin and markup in one sentence?

Markup is profit measured against what the job cost you; margin is profit measured against what the customer paid. Markup is always the larger number.

How do I convert a markup into a margin?

Margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup). A 50% markup is 0.5 ÷ 1.5 = 33.3% margin. To go the other way: markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin).

How do I price for a 50% margin?

Double your cost. Price = cost ÷ (1 − 0.5) = cost × 2. Note that this is a 100% markup — which is exactly why the two terms get confused.

Should I include my own salary in the cost?

Yes, at a market rate for the work you are doing. A business that is only profitable because the owner is unpaid is not profitable; it is a job with extra paperwork and no employment rights. Price your time at your real rate, then look at what is left over — that leftover is the actual profit of the business.

Why does my margin look fine but my bank account does not?

Three usual suspects: unbilled hours that never entered the cost, tax that was never set aside, and invoices that have not been paid. The tax set-aside calculator and the late fee calculator deal with the second and third.

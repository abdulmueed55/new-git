Can you actually charge a late fee?

In most countries, yes — but only if you can point to a term the client agreed to, or to a statute. Three routes exist, and they are not equally strong:

  • Your contract or your invoice terms. The strongest position. A line such as “overdue amounts accrue interest at 1.5% per month” in a signed agreement is enforceable in most jurisdictions. A line that appears for the first time on the invoice is weaker, because the client never agreed to it, but it is still worth having.
  • Statute. Several countries give you a right to interest even with no contract term at all. The UK's Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act and the EU Late Payment Directive both do this for business-to-business debts.
  • Nothing in writing anywhere. You can still ask, and clients often pay to keep the relationship. You just have little leverage if they refuse.

The three ways to calculate it

Monthly percentage

The freelancer standard: 1.5% per month, which is 18% a year. On a $2,500 invoice that is $37.50 a month, or $1.23 a day. Decide whether a part month counts as a whole month — charging a full month's interest on day 31 is legal if your terms say so, but prorating daily is much harder for a client to argue with, and this calculator does either.

Annual rate

Interest × days ÷ 365. Used where contracts quote an annual figure, and the basis for almost every statutory scheme.

UK statutory interest

For business-to-business debts, the rate is the Bank of England base rate plus 8 percentage points, calculated daily. You can also claim fixed compensation on top, as a flat sum per invoice:

Getting paid, not just getting owed

The fee is leverage, not income. Freelancers who get paid fastest tend to do the same four things:

  1. Put the term in the contract, not just the invoice, and say it out loud at kickoff so it is never a surprise.
  2. Chase on day one, politely. Most late payments are an unopened email or a missing purchase-order number, not a refusal to pay.
  3. Send the calculation, not a complaint. “As of today the balance is $2,592.50, including $92.50 of contractual interest” moves an invoice up the queue in a way that “just checking in” never does.
  4. Take a deposit next time. The project quote calculator works out a deposit amount for you. A client who will not pay 40% up front is telling you something useful.

Frequently asked questions

What is a reasonable late fee percentage?

1% to 1.5% per month is the usual range for freelancers and small agencies, equal to 12% to 18% a year. Going much higher risks a court treating the term as a penalty rather than genuine compensation, which can make it unenforceable. Some US states also cap the interest a business may charge, so check your state's usury rules before writing 2% or more into a contract.

Do I charge the late fee on the invoice total including sales tax or VAT?

Normally yes — the client owes the gross amount, so interest runs on the gross amount. Under the UK statutory scheme interest runs on the full debt including VAT. The fee itself is generally treated as compensation rather than a supply, so you usually do not add VAT to the interest; confirm with your accountant for your country.

When does the clock start?

The day after the payment due date, not the invoice date. If your terms are net 30 and you invoiced on 1 March, payment is due 31 March and interest starts 1 April. Where no terms were agreed, many jurisdictions default to 30 days after the client received the invoice or the goods, whichever is later.

Should I waive the fee if the client finally pays?

Often, yes — and say so explicitly: “I have waived the $92.50 interest this time.” You keep the relationship, and you have established that the fee is real, which makes it credible the next time. Waiving it silently teaches the client that the term is decorative.

Is this legal advice?

No. This is a calculator and a general explanation. Late payment rules vary by country, state and contract, and nothing here is a substitute for advice from a lawyer in your jurisdiction.

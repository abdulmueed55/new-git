How this calculator works

The arithmetic that sinks most freelancers is simple: they divide the salary they used to earn by 2,080 hours and call it a rate. That number is always too low, for three reasons. An employer pays for your holidays, your sick days and the hours you spend in meetings that nobody invoices. When you work for yourself, you pay for all of it.

So this tool runs the calculation in the order money actually moves:

  1. Start with take-home pay. The income figure you enter is what you want to keep, not what you want to invoice.
  2. Gross it up for tax. If you want to keep $60,000 and lose 28% to tax, you need $60,000 ÷ 0.72 = $83,333 of taxable profit, not $76,800. Grossing up and discounting are not the same operation, and this is where most back-of-envelope sums go wrong.
  3. Add business expenses. Your laptop, your accountant and your insurance come out of revenue, so revenue has to cover them.
  4. Add a profit buffer. Some invoices get paid late, some never get paid, and some projects run 20% over. A 10% cushion is not greed, it is survival.
  5. Divide by hours you can really bill. Not 40 a week. After holidays, public holidays, sick days, admin, sales calls, proposals and invoicing, 25 billable hours in a 40-hour week is a good week.

The formula

Billable hours per year = weekly billable hours × (52 − holiday weeks − public holidays ÷ 5 − sick days ÷ 5)

Revenue needed = (take-home ÷ (1 − tax rate)) + expenses

Hourly rate = revenue needed × (1 + profit buffer) ÷ billable hours

A worked example

Say you want $60,000 in your pocket, you spend $6,000 a year running the business, you lose 28% to tax, and you can bill 25 hours a week. You take four weeks off, eight public holidays and allow five sick days.

If you had used the naive method — $60,000 ÷ 2,080 — you would have charged $28.85 an hour and gone out of business inside two years.

What to do with the number

Treat it as a floor, not a ceiling. It is the rate below which you are subsidising your clients. Three things to remember when you quote it:

  • Round up, never down. $86.58 becomes $90. No client has ever walked away over 4%, and an odd number looks like you are negotiating against yourself.
  • Quote projects, not hours, once you can. Hourly billing punishes you for getting faster. Use this rate to price the project, then quote the project.
  • Raise it every year. Your costs rise. If your rate does not, you take a pay cut annually without noticing.

Frequently asked questions

What is a realistic number of billable hours per week?

Between 20 and 27 for most full-time freelancers. The rest of a 40-hour week goes to proposals, invoicing, email, bookkeeping, marketing and learning. Agencies plan for 60–70% utilisation on salaried staff and they have administrators; a solo freelancer doing their own admin should not plan for more.

Should I charge the same rate to every client?

No. The figure here is your minimum. Rush work, difficult stakeholders, unclear scope and high-stakes projects all justify a premium, and the calculator has a rush and complexity multiplier in the project quote tool for exactly that. What should never happen is going below this number, because then the work costs you money to do.

Does this work outside the United States?

Yes. The maths is country-neutral — pick your currency and enter your own combined tax rate. A UK sole trader would enter income tax plus Class 4 National Insurance; a German freelancer would enter Einkommensteuer plus their health insurance contribution; a Canadian would enter federal plus provincial plus CPP.

Why does the calculator add a profit buffer on top of my salary?

Because your salary is a cost of the business, not its profit. The buffer is what absorbs the invoice that gets paid 90 days late, the client that disappears, the project that runs over, and the new laptop you did not budget for. Freelancers who skip it end up funding those events out of their own take-home pay.

Is my data sent anywhere?

No. Every calculation on this site runs in your browser with JavaScript. Nothing you type is uploaded, stored or logged by us.

The gap nobody warns you about

The reason experienced freelancers tell beginners to charge double their old salary is not greed. It is this gap. An employee sees a gross salary and a net salary, and the distance between them is tax. A freelancer has four layers of distance:

  1. Business expenses come out before anything is yours.
  2. Self-employment tax — you pay both the employee and employer halves of social contributions. In the US that is 15.3% rather than 7.65%.
  3. Income tax, with no employer withholding it for you, which is why the bill feels so much larger even when the rate is identical.
  4. Benefits you now buy yourself: health insurance, retirement, paid leave, disability cover. An employer's contribution to these is typically worth 20–30% of salary.

Run the numbers and a freelancer invoicing $120,000 commonly lands somewhere near $65,000–$72,000 of genuine take-home pay. That is a good living. It is not $120,000.

How the calculation runs

Net profit = revenue − business expenses
SE tax = net profit × 92.35% × your social tax rate
Taxable income = net profit − half the SE tax − retirement − health premiums
Take-home = net profit − all taxes − retirement − health premiums

Two deliberate simplifications. First, income tax uses a single effective rate rather than progressive brackets, because an effective rate is the honest way to think about a whole year and brackets differ by country. Second, the retirement contribution is subtracted from take-home even though it remains your money — it is shown separately in the results so you can see both views. For precise self-employment tax including the wage cap, use the self-employment tax calculator.

What an effective tax rate actually is

Your marginal rate is what the next dollar costs. Your effective rate is total tax divided by total income, and it is always lower. A US freelancer in the 24% bracket typically has an effective federal rate near 14–17%, because the early portions of income were taxed at 10% and 12%. Entering your top bracket here will make the picture look much worse than reality — use last year's return to find your real effective rate.

Three ways to widen the gap in your favour

  • Claim every legitimate expense. Home office, phone, internet, software, courses, professional insurance, bank fees, accounting. Each dollar of genuine expense saves you both income tax and self-employment tax — roughly 30–40 cents on the dollar.
  • Use the retirement accounts you are entitled to. In the US a SEP-IRA or solo 401(k) lets a self-employed person shelter far more than a regular IRA. The contribution reduces taxable income today and stays yours.
  • Raise your rate before raising your hours. Ten percent more revenue at the same hours flows almost entirely to profit. Ten percent more hours costs you ten percent of your life. The hourly rate calculator shows where you should be.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my retirement contribution subtracted from take-home pay?

Because it is not money you can spend this month. It is still yours — which is why the results show it as its own figure. Think of take-home as spendable cash and the retirement line as savings you have already made, rather than money lost.

What effective income tax rate should I enter?

The most reliable source is last year's tax return: divide total tax by total income. If you have no return yet, a US freelancer with $80,000–$120,000 of net profit is often in the 13–18% effective federal range. Enter your top bracket and the result will be unduly pessimistic.

Does this work for UK, Canadian or Australian freelancers?

Yes, with your own rates. A UK sole trader would enter roughly 6% for Class 4 National Insurance and their effective income tax rate, with 0% state tax. A Canadian would use CPP contributions plus federal and provincial rates. An Australian sole trader would enter 0% for social tax and their effective rate, plus superannuation in the retirement field.

Should I use this to decide between freelancing and a job offer?

It is a good starting point, as long as you compare like with like. Against a $95,000 salary, compare your freelance take-home plus retirement plus health premiums, because the employer was paying for those too. Also price the things this calculator cannot: unpaid leave, income volatility, and the time you spend selling rather than working.

Is any of this stored or sent to a server?

No. The calculation is JavaScript running in your own browser. Nothing is transmitted, saved or logged.

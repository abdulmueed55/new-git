Why a separate account beats willpower

Nobody overspends their tax money on purpose. It happens because a $4,000 payment lands in the same account as everything else and, for three months, it looks like $4,000 of available money. By the time the quarterly payment is due, some of it is a new laptop.

The freelancers who never have this problem all do the same thing: a second savings account, and a standing habit that the moment a client payment clears, a fixed percentage moves out of reach. It takes thirty seconds and it removes an entire category of anxiety from your year.

What percentage should you set aside?

SituationSuggested set-aside
Low profit, no state income tax20–25%
Typical US freelancer, moderate state tax28–32%
High earner, or a high-tax state such as California or New York35–40%
UK sole trader, basic rate25–30%

Set it against profit, not revenue. If you invoice $10,000 and spend $2,500 running the business, the 30% applies to $7,500. Setting aside a percentage of revenue is simpler and over-saves, which is a pleasant problem; just be consistent about which one you are doing.

The quarterly deadlines

US estimated tax payments are due four times a year, usually on these dates (they shift to the next business day when one falls on a weekend or holiday):

QuarterIncome periodUsually due
Q11 January – 31 March15 April
Q21 April – 31 May15 June
Q31 June – 31 August15 September
Q41 September – 31 December15 January, next year

Note that the quarters are not equal lengths — Q2 covers two months and Q3 covers three. It is a quirk of the tax code, not a mistake.

The safe-harbour rule, which removes the guesswork

You cannot know this year's income in April. The US tax code solves that: if you pay at least 100% of last year's total tax across your four estimated payments — 110% if your prior-year adjusted gross income was above $150,000 — you cannot be charged an underpayment penalty, no matter how much you end up earning. Take last year's total tax, divide by four, and pay that. Any extra is settled in April.

First year freelancing? There is no prior year to copy, so estimate forwards and set aside generously. Over-saving costs you a little interest; under-saving costs you a penalty and a very unpleasant April.

Keep the money working

Tax money sitting in a current account earns nothing while you hold it for up to a year. A high-yield savings account or money market fund keeps it liquid and pays interest — on an average balance of $9,000 at 4%, that is a few hundred dollars a year for no work. Do not lock it up or invest it in anything that can fall; this money has a deadline.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to pay quarterly, or can I just pay in April?

In the US, if you expect to owe $1,000 or more in tax for the year, the IRS expects quarterly estimated payments. Paying it all in April means an underpayment penalty on top, calculated as interest on what you should have paid earlier. The penalty is not enormous, but it is entirely avoidable.

What if my income is wildly unpredictable?

Use the per-invoice mode and set a percentage aside from every payment as it arrives. Your savings then track your income automatically, with no forecasting. At each quarterly deadline, pay what the safe-harbour rule requires and leave the rest in the account.

Should I set aside from revenue or from profit?

Profit is correct, because that is what is taxed. Setting aside from revenue is simpler and deliberately over-saves, which many freelancers prefer in their first couple of years. Pick one and apply it the same way every time.

Does this apply outside the United States?

The percentage logic applies everywhere; the deadlines do not. UK self-assessment uses payments on account due 31 January and 31 July. Canada uses 15 March, June, September and December. Australia uses quarterly BAS or PAYG instalments. Set your own reminders for your country's dates.

Where should I keep the money?

A separate high-yield savings account at a bank different from your main one — the extra friction is a feature. Keep it liquid and keep it safe. Tax money is not an investment fund; it has a due date and it is not really yours.

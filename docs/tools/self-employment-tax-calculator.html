What self-employment tax actually is

An employee and their employer each pay 7.65% of wages into Social Security and Medicare. When you are self-employed you are both parties, so you pay the full 15.3%. This is separate from and in addition to federal income tax — which is why a freelancer earning $80,000 can be shocked by a tax bill that an employee earning $80,000 never sees.

It breaks into three pieces:

ComponentRateApplies to
Social Security (OASDI)12.4%Earnings up to the annual wage base, then nothing
Medicare2.9%Every dollar, no ceiling
Additional Medicare0.9%Earnings above $200k single / $250k married filing jointly

The 92.35% rule nobody explains

You do not pay SE tax on your full net profit. You pay it on 92.35% of it. The reasoning is that an employer's half of payroll tax is a deductible business expense, so the law gives the self-employed an equivalent adjustment by shrinking the base. On $80,000 of profit, the taxable base is $73,880, and 15.3% of that is $11,303.64 — not $12,240.

SE tax base = net profit × 0.9235
Social Security = min(SE base, wage base − W-2 wages) × 12.4%
Medicare = SE base × 2.9%
Additional Medicare = (SE base + W-2 wages − threshold) × 0.9%

The wage cap, and why a side job changes your bill

Social Security stops at a ceiling that the Social Security Administration resets every year. Above it, you keep paying Medicare but the 12.4% disappears — so very high earners have a lower effective SE rate than middle earners.

The catch that surprises people with both a job and a side business: your W-2 wages fill that cap first. If you already earned $150,000 at a job and the cap is $184,500, only $34,500 of your freelance profit faces Social Security tax. Enter your W-2 wages above and the calculator handles it.

Half of it comes back as a deduction

You can deduct one half of your self-employment tax when calculating your adjusted gross income. It is an above-the-line deduction, so you get it whether or not you itemise. The 0.9% Additional Medicare surtax is not deductible. The calculator shows the deductible figure separately — it typically knocks a few hundred to a few thousand dollars off your income tax.

Paying it: quarterly, not in April

The IRS expects estimated payments four times a year, generally 15 April, 15 June, 15 September and 15 January. Miss them and you owe an underpayment penalty even if you pay the full amount later. The safest approach for most freelancers is the safe-harbour rule: pay 100% of last year's total tax (110% if your prior-year AGI was over $150,000) and you cannot be penalised, however well this year goes.

Frequently asked questions

Do I owe self-employment tax if I only made a few thousand dollars?

You owe it once your net earnings from self-employment reach $400 for the year. Below that threshold there is no SE tax, although the income may still be reportable for income tax.

Is self-employment tax the same as income tax?

No, and this is the single most expensive misunderstanding in freelancing. SE tax funds Social Security and Medicare and is calculated on your business profit. Income tax is calculated separately on your taxable income after deductions. You pay both.

Does forming an S corporation reduce this?

It can. An S corp lets you split your profit into a reasonable salary (which pays payroll tax) and a distribution (which does not). The saving only outweighs the payroll, filing and accounting costs at a certain profit level — commonly cited as somewhere above $80,000 to $100,000 of net profit, but it depends on your state and your reasonable salary. Talk to a CPA before restructuring.

Why is the wage base an editable field?

Because the Social Security Administration raises it every year with average wage growth, and a hard-coded figure in a calculator goes stale the moment it changes. Check the current year's number on the SSA website and type it in — the rest of the maths adapts.

What if I had a loss this year?

No self-employment tax is due on a loss. The calculator needs a positive net profit to produce a figure. A business loss may offset other income for income-tax purposes, which is worth raising with a tax preparer.

Adding tax and removing tax are different sums

Adding is intuitive: £1,000 plus 20% VAT is £1,200. Removing is where almost everyone slips. To find the net amount inside a £1,200 total, you do not subtract 20%. Twenty percent of £1,200 is £240, which would leave £960 — and £960 plus 20% is £1,152, not £1,200.

Adding: gross = net × (1 + rate)
Removing: net = gross ÷ (1 + rate)

At 20%, the correct shortcut for removing VAT is to divide by 1.2, or to multiply by 0.8333 — never to subtract 20%.

The error is small on one invoice and expensive across a year of bookkeeping. On £120,000 of gross income, subtracting instead of dividing overstates your VAT by £4,000.

VAT, GST, HST and sales tax are not the same thing

SystemWhereHow it works
VATUK, EU, much of the worldCharged at each stage; businesses reclaim what they paid
GST / HSTAustralia, NZ, Canada, India, SingaporeSame principle, different name
Sales taxUnited StatesCharged once, at the final sale, by state and often city

The practical difference for a freelancer: under VAT or GST, the tax you charge was never your money — you are collecting it on the government's behalf and will hand it over. Under US sales tax, most professional services are not taxable at all in most states, though software and digital products increasingly are.

The mistake that ruins a cash flow

You invoice £12,000 including VAT. £12,000 arrives in your account. It looks like a good month. But £2,000 of it belongs to HMRC and will be collected at the end of the quarter.

Freelancers who register for VAT and then spend their VAT are one of the most common casualties in self-employment, and the fix is the same mechanical habit that works for income tax: a separate account, funded the day the money arrives. The quarterly tax set-aside calculator does the same job for income tax and self-employment tax.

Should you register?

Thresholds differ by country, and crossing one is usually mandatory rather than optional. Two points worth knowing before you get there:

  • If your clients are VAT-registered businesses, charging VAT costs them nothing — they reclaim it. Registration can even make you look more established.
  • If your clients are consumers, registration makes you 20% more expensive overnight, or 20% poorer if you absorb it. This is the point at which many small businesses deliberately slow their growth, which is rarely the right trade.

Registering voluntarily before you must lets you reclaim VAT on equipment and software. Whether that is worth the filing burden depends on what you spend; it is a good question for an accountant.

Frequently asked questions

How do I remove 20% VAT from a price?

Divide the gross amount by 1.2. A £1,200 total contains £1,000 net and £200 of VAT. Subtracting 20% is wrong and will understate your income and overstate your VAT.

Do freelancers in the United States charge sales tax?

Usually not on professional services such as design, development, writing or consulting, though a minority of states do tax some of them. Software, SaaS and digital goods are taxed in a growing number of states. Rules are set state by state and sometimes city by city, so check your own state's department of revenue.

What rate should I use for an international client?

It depends on where the client is and whether they are a business. Within the EU, business-to-business sales across borders usually shift the VAT obligation to the customer under the reverse charge, so you charge 0% and note it on the invoice. Selling to consumers abroad is far more complicated and often requires charging their local rate. Get advice before assuming.

Is the tax I charge part of my income?

No. VAT and GST you collect are never yours — you are holding them for the tax authority. Your income is the net figure. Treating gross receipts as income is how people end up unable to pay a quarterly VAT bill.

Can I use this for discounts or tips as well?

The arithmetic is identical for any percentage added to or removed from a figure, so yes. Enter the percentage in the rate field and pick the direction you need.

Your salary was never your compensation

When a company says a role pays $85,000, the amount leaving their accounts is far higher. They also pay their half of payroll taxes, your health insurance, a retirement match, your laptop, your software licences, your desk, your training budget and your insurance. In the United States, employers commonly budget 1.25 to 1.4 times salary as the true cost of an employee.

They also paid you for the days you did not work. Twenty-five days of holiday is five weeks of salary you received while producing nothing. As a freelancer, those five weeks cost you income instead of providing it.

The two mistakes stacked on top of each other

Mistake one: using salary instead of package

$85,000 of salary is really $85,000 + 25% benefits + 7.65% employer taxes = $112,752 of value. Converting from the smaller number undershoots by a third before you have even started.

Mistake two: dividing by 2,080

There are 2,080 working hours in a year, but you cannot bill them. Sales calls, proposals, invoicing, bookkeeping, email, admin and learning are all real work that no client pays for. A realistic figure is 1,100 to 1,300 billable hours a year. Dividing by 2,080 instead of 1,150 understates your rate by roughly 45%.

Put together, these two errors compound: the naive answer is often less than half the correct one. That is the entire explanation for why so many people leave a stable job, work harder than ever, and end the year poorer.

True package = salary × (1 + benefits% + employer tax%)
Billable hours = weekly billable hours × (52 − weeks off)
Equivalent rate = (true package + your business expenses) ÷ billable hours

Things this number still does not include

It replaces your old package. It does not pay you for the new risks you have taken on:

  • Gaps between projects. Nobody pays you to look for work.
  • Clients who disappear. An employer's payroll does not simply fail to run.
  • No severance, no unemployment cover, no sick pay.
  • Volatility. A good year and a bad year average out on paper and do not average out on a mortgage application.

Most experienced freelancers add 15–25% on top of the equivalent rate for exactly these reasons. Use this converter for the floor, then the hourly rate calculator to build the rate you actually want, and the take-home pay calculator to confirm what lands in your account.

Frequently asked questions

Is it true that freelancers should charge double their old hourly wage?

The rule of thumb is roughly right and for the wrong reasons, which is why people apply it badly. The doubling is not a premium for being self-employed; it is the arithmetic of lost benefits, employer taxes, unpaid time off and non-billable hours. Run your own numbers and the multiple usually lands between 2.2 and 2.8.

What percentage should I use for benefits?

Twenty to thirty percent covers most employees in the US and Western Europe. Use the higher end if you had good health cover, a generous retirement match, or an employer who paid for equipment and training. If you genuinely had no benefits at all, enter 0 — but check first, because paid leave and employer taxes still count and are entered separately.

Why does the calculator show the value of my paid time off separately?

Because it is the part people find hardest to believe. Twenty-five paid days off is roughly 9.6% of a working year — on $85,000 that is about $8,170 you were paid for not working. It is shown for context rather than added twice; the rate already covers it, because the billable hours figure excludes the weeks you take off.

Should I quote this rate to clients, or is it just for me?

It is a floor for your own decision-making. Quote projects rather than hours wherever you can, using the project quote calculator. Clients buy outcomes; hourly rates invite them to audit your speed instead of valuing your result.

Does this work in the UK, Canada or Australia?

Yes — change the currency and the employer tax percentage. UK employer National Insurance is higher than US employer FICA, Australian employers pay compulsory superannuation on top of salary, and Canadian employers pay CPP and EI contributions. The structure of the calculation is identical everywhere.

Your First Year Freelancing: A Tax Checklist

The eight things to set up in your first year of self-employment so that tax season is an administrative task rather than a financial emergency.

Published 11 October 2026

Advertisement

The first year of freelancing has a predictable shape: the work goes better than expected and the paperwork goes worse. Almost all of the damage comes from a handful of things not set up in month one. Here is the list, in the order that matters.

1. Open a separate business account

Before anything else. Not because the law demands it for a sole trader in most countries, but because mixing personal and business money turns bookkeeping into archaeology. One account in, one card out, and your records build themselves. It also makes the next item possible.

2. Open a second savings account for tax

The single highest-value habit in self-employment: the day a client payment clears, a fixed percentage moves to an account you do not touch. Twenty-five to thirty percent suits most freelancers; thirty-five to forty if you are in a high-tax state or bracket. Use the quarterly tax set-aside calculator to set the percentage against your expected profit.

People who do this find tax season boring. People who do not find it existential.

3. Understand that you now pay two taxes

This is the one that blindsides nearly everyone leaving employment. In the United States you owe income tax and self-employment tax — 15.3% covering both halves of Social Security and Medicare, because you are now both the employer and the employee. It is calculated on your business profit before income tax is considered at all.

A freelancer with $80,000 of net profit owes roughly $11,300 in self-employment tax before a cent of income tax. Run your own figure through the self-employment tax calculator, which applies the 92.35% adjustment and the Social Security wage cap correctly.

Outside the US the structure differs but the principle holds: the UK has Class 2 and Class 4 National Insurance, Canada has CPP contributions at the self-employed rate, Australia has compulsory superannuation. Find out which applies to you in month one, not month eleven.

4. Diarise the quarterly deadlines

Most countries want tax during the year, not after it. US estimated payments are generally due 15 April, 15 June, 15 September and 15 January. The UK uses payments on account on 31 January and 31 July. Missing them means penalties even if you eventually pay in full.

If you cannot forecast your income — and in year one, you cannot — use the safe-harbour rule: pay 100% of last year's total tax (110% if your prior-year income was above $150,000) and you cannot be penalised, however much you end up earning.

5. Track expenses from day one

Every legitimate business expense reduces both your income tax and your self-employment tax, which in practice means each dollar of expense saves roughly 30–40 cents. Commonly missed items:

  • Home office — a proportion of rent, utilities and internet
  • Phone, at the business-use percentage
  • Software and subscriptions, including the small monthly ones that add up to thousands
  • Professional development: courses, books, conferences
  • Professional insurance and liability cover
  • Bank charges, payment processor fees and currency conversion costs
  • Accountancy and legal fees
  • Equipment, whether deducted at once or depreciated

Photograph receipts as they happen. Reconstructing a year of expenses in April reliably loses you money, because you will forget things you genuinely paid for.

6. Price so that tax is already covered

The most common first-year mistake is not a tax mistake at all — it is a pricing mistake that only shows up at tax time. If you set your rate by looking at your old salary, tax was never in the number. Use the hourly rate calculator, which starts from what you want to take home and grosses it up for tax and expenses, so the bill in April was already paid for in every invoice you sent.

7. Get a contract, and put a late fee in it

A two-page agreement covering scope, revisions, payment terms and a late fee clause prevents more first-year disasters than any other document. “Overdue amounts accrue interest at 1.5% per month” costs you nothing to include and changes how your invoices are treated. The invoice late fee calculator works out what to put on the reminder when you need it.

8. Talk to an accountant once, early

Not monthly bookkeeping — one paid consultation in your first few months. An hour with someone who knows your country's rules typically pays for itself many times over: the right structure, the deductions you are missing, the retirement account you are entitled to, the registration thresholds you are approaching. Doing this in month three is cheap. Doing it after a mistake is not.

The honest summary

None of this is difficult. It is just unfamiliar, and it all arrives at once while you are also trying to find clients and do the actual work. Set up the two accounts, pick a set-aside percentage, diarise four dates, and keep your receipts. That is roughly 90% of the benefit, and it takes an afternoon.

Then see what you actually keep with the take-home pay calculator — knowing the real number is what turns freelancing from a gamble into a business.

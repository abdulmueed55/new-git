Why the rate is only half the question

Knowing that a country charges 21% tells you almost nothing about what you owe. What decides your position is the turnover at which registration stops being optional, whether a small-business exemption applies to you, and where a supply is treated as happening when your client sits somewhere else.

That last point catches out nearly every freelancer with foreign clients. Selling a service across an EU border to another registered business usually shifts the obligation to the buyer under the reverse charge, so you invoice without adding anything and explain why on the invoice. Selling the same service to a private individual can require their local rate instead. Getting it the wrong way round means either an unexpected bill or an invoice your client refuses to pay.

Thresholds move, and so do rates

Several countries listed here changed their standard rate within the last few years, and more have adjusted the point at which you must register. Treat every figure as a starting point to confirm rather than as an authority. Each page names the body that actually collects the money, which is where the current number lives.

Whatever the rate, it is not income

The money you add to an invoice was never yours. It sits in your account looking like a good month until the return falls due. Freelancers who treat it as revenue are the ones who cannot pay at the end of the quarter, and the fix is the same separate-account habit that works for income tax.
